Combinatorics

Basic definitions

All notes Graph theory Pure mathematics Combinatorics Computer science Recreational

Combinatorics asks how many, whether any, and how they sit together. Counting, existence, and structure. Graph theory is one of its languages; the notes below are the first vocabulary that is not already on the graph theory page.

1. What is being counted
Combinatorics
The mathematics of finite configurations: selections, arrangements, and designs. Three typical questions: how many objects of a given kind exist; whether at least one exists; what the extremal size is.
Counting, existence, structure
Counting produces a number. Existence produces a yes or a no, often by construction or by a counting argument that forbids zero. Structure asks what the objects look like: unique, many, or forced into a particular shape.
Finite set
A set \( X \) with \( |X| = n < \infty \). Most of the formulae below are for finite sets. The empty set has size \( 0 \).
2. Sets, subsets, and binomial coefficients
Power set
The power set \( \mathcal{P}(X) \) is the set of all subsets of \( X \). If \( |X| = n \), then \( |\mathcal{P}(X)| = 2^n \).
Binomial coefficient
\( \binom{n}{k} \) is the number of \( k \)-element subsets of an \( n \)-element set. \[ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!} \qquad (0 \le k \le n). \] Also \( \binom{n}{k} = 0 \) when \( k < 0 \) or \( k > n \), and \( \binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n} = 1 \).
Pascal's identity
A \( k \)-subset of \( \{1,\ldots,n\} \) either contains \( n \) or it does not: \[ \binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k} + \binom{n-1}{k-1}. \]
Binomial theorem
\[ (x+y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} x^{n-k} y^{k}. \] The row sums of Pascal's triangle are the powers of two: \( \sum_k \binom{n}{k} = 2^n \).
3. Permutations and combinations
Permutation
An ordered arrangement of \( n \) distinct objects. There are \( n! \) of them. The number of injective maps from a \( k \)-set into an \( n \)-set is \( P(n,k) = n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = n!/(n-k)! \).
Combination
An unordered selection. The number of ways to choose \( k \) objects from \( n \) is \( \binom{n}{k} \). Order is forgotten: \( P(n,k) = k!\,\binom{n}{k} \).
With repetition
The number of ways to choose \( k \) elements from \( n \) types, repetition allowed, order ignored, is \( \binom{n+k-1}{k} \). This is the “stars and bars” count of non-negative integer solutions to \( x_1 + \cdots + x_n = k \).
Multinomial coefficient
The number of ways to partition \( n \) distinct objects into labelled groups of sizes \( n_1,\ldots,n_r \) with \( n_1+\cdots+n_r = n \) is \( n!/(n_1! \cdots n_r!) \).
4. Pigeonhole principle
Pigeonhole
If \( n+1 \) pigeons occupy \( n \) holes, some hole contains at least two pigeons. More generally: if \( n \) objects are placed in \( k \) boxes, some box contains at least \( \lceil n/k \rceil \) objects.
Why it is useful
It proves existence without exhibiting the object. In any simple graph on \( n \ge 2 \) vertices, two vertices have the same degree: the possible degrees are \( 0,1,\ldots,n-1 \), but \( 0 \) and \( n-1 \) cannot occur together, so there are at most \( n-1 \) possible values for \( n \) vertices.
A court of pigeons
The same principle is a writing prompt as much as a lemma. The mathematics is the collision; the story is who is forced to share a hole.
5. Double counting and bijections
Bijection
A one-to-one correspondence. If there is a bijection between two finite sets, they have the same size. Many identities are proved by exhibiting such a map rather than by algebra.
Double counting
Count the same collection in two ways. The two expressions are equal. The handshaking lemma is the graph-theoretic instance: counting vertex–edge incidences from vertices and from edges gives \( \sum_v d(v) = 2|E| \).
Complementary counting
Count the objects that fail the condition, and subtract from the total. Often cleaner than building the good objects directly.
6. Inclusion-exclusion
Two sets
\[ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B|. \]
Three sets
Add the three sizes, subtract the three double intersections, add back the triple intersection. The pattern continues: add the odd intersections, subtract the even ones.
Derangements
A derangement is a permutation with no fixed point. Inclusion-exclusion gives the count \[ n! \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}, \] which is the nearest integer to \( n!/e \) for \( n \ge 1 \).
7. Graphs as combinatorics

A graph is a finite set of vertices together with a set of unordered pairs. Once that is granted, extremal questions appear immediately.

How many edges?
A simple graph on \( n \) vertices has at most \( \binom{n}{2} \) edges, with equality only for \( K_n \).
Mantel's theorem
A triangle-free simple graph on \( n \) vertices has at most \( \lfloor n^2/4 \rfloor \) edges. Equality holds for the complete bipartite graph \( K_{\lfloor n/2 \rfloor, \lceil n/2 \rceil} \). This is the first theorem of extremal graph theory; Turán's theorem extends it from triangles to \( K_r \).
Ramsey, in one sentence
Complete disorder is impossible: in any sufficiently large complete graph whose edges are coloured red and blue, a monochromatic triangle (and, later, a monochromatic \( K_r \)) is forced.
8. Existence versus construction
Existence
A counting or probabilistic argument may show that an object exists without producing it. The probabilistic method is the organised form of this idea.
Construction
An explicit example. Constructions are harder, and they travel: a single graph can be reused as a counterexample in several conjectures.
Uniqueness
Sometimes there is exactly one object of a given kind. A uniquely Hamiltonian graph is the graph-theoretic instance: exactly one cycle through every vertex. Existence, construction, and uniqueness are three different kinds of answer.

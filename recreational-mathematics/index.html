A Marvellous Nonsense

Sachal Abdullah · June 2026

Essay (PDF)

Lewis Carroll describes Snarks to be these elusive creatures who you should fear, and keep an eye open for, especially if it is the Boojum— the most dangerous of the Snarks, a threat to your existence. This existential dread induced by a Snark becomes clearer in Martin Gardner’s The Annotated Snark, where he equates the Snark with humanity’s endless search for meaning— which is agonizing and elusive in the same way.

The first is the taste,

Which is meagre and hollow, but crisp:

Like a coat that is rather too tight in the waist,

With a flavour of Will-o’-the-wisp.

In 1976, Gardner bridged his love for Lewis Carroll and recreational mathematics by popularizing the term Snark in an edition of Mathematical Games, namely: Snarks, Boojums and other conjectures related to the four-color-map theorem.

Its habit of getting up late you’ll agree

That it carries too far, when I say

That it frequently breakfasts at five-o’clock tea,

And dines on the following day.

Just like the ship’s crew in the poem, the hunt for the Snark is just as important for graph theorists. It is deeply challenging, and whenever a Snark is found, it is a significant result for the field.

The third is its slowness in taking a jest.

Should you happen to venture on one,

It will sigh like a thing that is deeply distressed:

And it always looks grave at a pun.

What is a Snark for a graph theorist after all? In order to understand that, you must leave your Cartesian Coordinates behind; with nothing but dots and lines, arrangements emerge that will blow your mind.

The fourth is its fondness for bathing-machines,

Which it constantly carries about,

And believes that they add to the beauty of scenes—

A sentiment open to doubt

A Snark is an object in graph theory, which has a specific structure where every vertex (node) has three edges (lines) coming out of it, each connected to a distinct vertex. And to colour its edges, it requires at least 4 colours such that no two adjacent edges share the same colour. We can see these properties in the Petersen graph: The Petersen graph was the first Snark discovered by Julius Petersen in 1898, and it remained so for the next 50 years. Afterwards, when more Snarks were being discovered, Gardner proposed to name them Snarks rather than nontrivial uncolorable trivalent graphs.

The fifth is ambition. It next will be right

To describe each particular batch:

Distinguishing those that have feathers, and bite,

From those that have whiskers, and scratch.

The next Snarks are the Blanuša Snarks discovered by a Croatian mathematician, Danilo Blanuša, in 1946. One of them uses 18 vertices and the other one uses 26 vertices. The Blanuša Snark on 18 vertices is the 2nd smallest Snark after the Petersen graph. This Snark was first popularised by the Croatian mathematics society in 1996, then later it appeared on a Croatian stamp around the turn of the millennium, as the year 2000 was declared World Mathematical Year at an IMU conference (Rio de Janeiro, 1992); other countries also participated with their mathematical stamps. We don’t know what is the Boojum of graph theory, but we know what it has to look like— the hunt goes on... The Boojum has all the properties of a Snark with one additional property— when you draw its edges they should not cross or overlap; not even a pair of edges! Such graphs are called planar graphs. The Boojum is a planar Snark.

For, although common Snarks do no manner of harm,

Yet, I feel it my duty to say,

Some are Boojums— ” The Bellman broke off in

alarm,

If a Boojum exists it must look something like the following graph. It does not have any crossing edges, all the vertices are of degree 3 but, its edges can be coloured with three colours, hence it is not a Snark or a Boojum. I invite you to try colouring its edges.

Come, listen, my men, while I tell you again

The five unmistakable marks

By which you may know, wheresoever you go,

The warranted genuine Snarks

But where is the nonsense, or what is nonsense? Apart from the stylistic choice of nonsense, everything about Snarks seems sensible and reasonable. Doesn’t it?

Carroll wrote this poem during the Victorian era when literature was supposed to teach moral values to kids, tell them how to behave and inculcate discipline. Carroll’s free-spirited writing broke those traditions and paved the way for more imaginative things. Before graph theory was a proper discipline, the Bourbaki style of writing, with dense rigour and formality was common. There was a deliberate exclusion of diagrams and graph theory was considered merely “parlour games” or “recreations”.

Gardner himself sat at exactly this intersection, a man who wrote about mathematics in newspapers, and celebrated the communal joy of mathematics. Perhaps we can say Gardner rescued the Snark, refusing to let graph theorists hide behind impenetrable terminology.

Am I the nonsense for drawing this parallel? Will I be exonerated of my informalities?

The weather of Prague is about to make everything melt,

when you plan to cool down

in your chill bath, keep your eyes open,

you may find a Snark.

Prompts

Written for Scribbles and Giggles, a creative writing event.

  1. You have found something inconclusive and arbitrary, how do you turn it around to fall in love again?
  2. A sea turtle has been connecting dots, going slower and slower but he is going to find the unique way back to his home after swimming around in the ocean.
  3. You have found a typewriter that writes without any limits- with an infinite strip of paper, but it only has three buttons: left, right and rest.
  4. A court of pigeons is in session, discussing their housing matters as they are growing in numbers, waiting for an order- a pigeonhole principle, to fit all of them.
  5. Each wife of Fibonacci,
    Eating nothing that wasn't starchy,
    Weighed as much as the two before her.
    His fifth was some signora! -J. A. LINDON
  6. A convicted person was asked to draw routes on a map to show how the heist was planned. It turns out the plot could be drawn without lifting a pencil.
  7. When do we need Venn diagrams?
  8. "More laughter, of course, does not necessarily mean funnier: nitrous oxide is not funny."
  9. It's a bird, Its plane.. No, It's a palindrome.
  10. When all of your knights are gone missing but their tours are interesting. As if they have left a trail, for you to find something.
  11. Between 1 and -1, there are numbers more than what you can understand for sure. Real will show you a mirror, imaginary will drown your sanity. And surreal will eat your fear of the both.

The mathematics

Snark
A connected bridgeless cubic graph whose edges need four colours: no two adjacent edges the same colour. Petersen (1898) was the first; the Blanuša snarks (1946) are the next smallest. Gardner named them, from Carroll. They appear in the research on Fleischner’s construction.
Boojum
In Gardner’s joke, a planar snark. The four colour theorem says there are none: a planar cubic bridgeless graph is 3-edge-colourable.
Unique path
A path that is the only walk of its kind between two vertices, or the only way home in a maze of dots. Uniqueness is the same question, later, as a uniquely Hamiltonian cycle.
Infinite tape
Three buttons — left, right, rest — on an infinite strip is a machine with a finite set of instructions and an unbounded memory. The writing is unlimited; the alphabet of moves is not.
Pigeonhole principle
\( n+1 \) pigeons and \( n \) holes: some hole holds at least two. Existence without a construction. The combinatorics notes record the usual form.
Fibonacci
\( F_n = F_{n-1} + F_{n-2} \), with a start such as \( F_1 = 1 \), \( F_2 = 1 \). Lindon's limerick is that recurrence as weight.
Eulerian trail
A trail that uses every edge once. A connected graph has a closed one iff every degree is even, and an open one iff exactly two vertices have odd degree. Drawing a plot without lifting the pencil is the same condition.
Venn diagram
Overlapping regions for membership in sets. Inclusion and exclusion made visible; the counting form is on the combinatorics page.
Palindrome
A sequence equal to its reverse. Combinatorics on words: how many, and under what symmetries.
Knight's tour
Visit every square of a board by knight moves. That is a Hamiltonian path in the knight's graph; a closed tour is a Hamiltonian cycle. The graph theory notes have the cycle vocabulary.
Between \(-1\) and \(1\)
The real interval is already infinite. Imaginary numbers adjoin a square root of \(-1\). Conway's surreal numbers include the reals and also infinities and infinitesimals.

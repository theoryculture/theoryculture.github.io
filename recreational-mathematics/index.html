Recreational Mathematics

Puzzles that became theorems

Recreational mathematics is not a lesser subject. Several of the central words in graph theory — Eulerian, Hamiltonian, uniquely Hamiltonian — began as games: a walk through a city, a knight on a board, a dodecahedron you could hold. The basic definitions are recorded separately. Here are the puzzles that made those words necessary.

1. The seven bridges of Königsberg

Königsberg (now Kaliningrad) had seven bridges joining two islands to the banks of the Pregel. The puzzle: walk through the city crossing each bridge exactly once.

The graph
Land masses become vertices; bridges become edges. The result is a connected multigraph with four vertices of odd degree.
Euler's theorem (1736)
A connected graph has a closed trail through every edge if and only if every degree is even. It has an open trail through every edge if and only if exactly two vertices have odd degree. Königsberg has four odd vertices, so no such walk exists.
What the puzzle invented
The distinction between a picture of a city and the abstract graph, and the first degree condition in graph theory. An Eulerian circuit is still named for this walk.
2. Knight's tours

A knight on a chessboard moves in an L: two squares one way and one square perpendicular. A tour visits every square exactly once. A closed tour returns to the start.

The knight's graph
Vertices are the squares. Two vertices are adjacent when a knight can move from one to the other in a single leap. A tour is a Hamiltonian path in this graph; a closed tour is a Hamiltonian cycle.
Which boards?
Closed tours exist on the usual \( 8 \times 8 \) board. They fail on boards that are too small, or whose colouring and degree sequence obstruct a cycle. The question “does a tour exist?” is already a Hamiltonian problem, and it is sensitive to the board.
Missing knights
Remove squares and the graph changes. Leftover tours — Hamiltonian paths on the punctured board — are a different puzzle, and a different existence question.
3. Hamilton's Icosian game

In 1857 William Rowan Hamilton sold a game on the dodecahedron: visit every vertex exactly once and return. The solid has twenty vertices, each of degree three. The game is a Hamiltonian cycle on that graph, held in the hand.

The dodecahedral graph
Twenty vertices, thirty edges, cubic and Hamiltonian. The research page draws one such cycle. Unlike the Eulerian case, there is no simple degree test that decides whether a graph has a Hamiltonian cycle.
Why the name stuck
Euler's problem is about edges; Hamilton's is about vertices. The two notions are easy to confuse and mathematically far apart. A graph may have one and not the other.
4. Drawings without lifting the pencil
House, envelope, heist
Children's drawings — a house with an X, an envelope — are Eulerian-path puzzles. A route through a city that traces every street once is the same problem with a story attached.
The test
Count odd-degree vertices. Zero: you may start anywhere and close. Two: start at one odd vertex and end at the other. More than two: put the pencil down; the drawing cannot be done in one stroke.
5. When a puzzle becomes a theorem
Recreation
A single instance: this city, this board, this solid. The pleasure is in finding the walk, or in seeing why none exists.
Mathematics
The instance is replaced by a class. Which graphs have an Eulerian circuit? Which graphs have a Hamiltonian cycle? Which have exactly one? The last question is uniqueness, and it is no longer a game.
Uniquely Hamiltonian
A graph with exactly one Hamiltonian cycle. Cycle graphs are the trivial examples. Whether a regular simple graph of degree at least \( 4 \) can be uniquely Hamiltonian is Sheehan's conjecture, still open in the even-regular case. The vocabulary is on the graph theory page; the research question sits on research.
6. Other rooms in the same house
Map colouring
Four colours suffice for planar maps. The four colour theorem is a recreational question with a long, un-recreational proof.
Instant Insanity and sliding puzzles
Cube-colouring puzzles and the 15-puzzle are configuration graphs: vertices are states, edges are legal moves. Solvability is connectivity in that graph, sometimes with a parity invariant.
Lewis Carroll
Word ladders, doublets, and logic games sit next to graph theory more closely than they first appear: a change of one letter is an edge. Recreation is a way into the definitions, not a substitute for them.

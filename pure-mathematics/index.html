Pure Mathematics

Definition, theorem, conjecture, proof

Pure mathematics asks what follows from the definitions, not what a machine should do next. Graph theory and combinatorics sit in that tradition: finite configurations, existence, uniqueness, and the shape of a proof. The graph theory and combinatorics notes are the vocabulary; this page is the surrounding language of proof. The computational side is on computer science.

1. What is being asked
Pure mathematics
The study of mathematical objects for their own structure: numbers, sets, graphs, groups, spaces. A result is valued because it is true and because the argument is the right one, not because it immediately computes a schedule or a shortest path.
Discrete mathematics
Mathematics of finite, or at least countable, configurations. Combinatorics, graph theory, and finite set theory are the usual core. Analysis and geometry are not absent from a pure mathematician's education, but the research on this site is discrete.
Combinatorial graph theory
Graphs treated as combinatorial objects: vertices, edges, cycles, colourings, decompositions. Not the same question as “how fast can an algorithm find a cycle,” though the two questions talk to each other. See Graph theory.
2. Definition, theorem, lemma, conjecture
Definition
A name for a class of objects, fixed precisely enough that two people can agree whether a given object belongs. A graph is a pair \( (V,E) \). A Hamiltonian cycle is a circuit through every vertex. Uniquely Hamiltonian means exactly one such cycle.
Theorem
A statement that has been proved. Euler's theorem on Eulerian circuits is a theorem: a connected graph has a closed trail through every edge if and only if every degree is even.
Lemma
A theorem whose main use is to prove another theorem. The name is about the role in an argument, not about size.
Conjecture
A statement believed, or at least seriously asked, but not proved. Sheehan's conjecture (1975) is one: there are no uniquely Hamiltonian \( r \)-regular simple graphs for \( r > 2 \). The odd-regular case is settled; the even-regular case remains open for \( 4 \le r \le 20 \).
Proof
A finite sequence of inferences from the definitions and from theorems already granted. A picture can suggest a proof. It is not a proof until the steps can be written.
Counterexample
A single object that kills a general claim. In graph theory a well-chosen snark, or a small multigraph, often does more work than a long calculation.
3. Existence, construction, uniqueness
Existence
There is at least one object of a given kind. The pigeonhole principle and the probabilistic method prove existence without exhibiting the object. See Combinatorics.
Construction
An explicit example. Fleischner's construction starts from a cubic hypohamiltonian graph and aims at a uniquely Hamiltonian 4-regular multigraph. A construction that works on Petersen may fail on the Blanuša snarks; that failure is itself a theorem about the reach of the method. See Research.
Uniqueness
There is exactly one. A uniquely Hamiltonian graph is the graph-theoretic instance: exactly one cycle through every vertex. Uniqueness is a different claim from existence, and a different claim from “unique up to automorphism.”
4. Open problems

An open problem is a well-posed question with no proof yet. Graph theory is full of them. Two that organise the notes:

Sheehan's conjecture
No uniquely Hamiltonian regular simple graph of degree greater than 2. Settled for odd degree (Thomason). Open for even degree in a wide range. Bounds exist: Thomassen for very large degree; Haxell–Seamone–Verstraëte down to degree greater than 22.
Sabidussi's compatibility conjecture
In a connected Eulerian graph of minimum degree 4, every Eulerian circuit admits a compatible cycle decomposition. The language — transitions, half-edges, \( X_T \) and \( X_S \) — is recorded under Graph theory.
5. Pure mathematics and computer science
The same objects, different questions
A Hamiltonian cycle is a combinatorial object. Asking whether an arbitrary graph has one is also a decision problem, and that problem is NP-complete. The first question is pure mathematics; the second is computer science. Both are about the same cycle.
Why the distinction matters
A polynomial algorithm for Eulerian circuits does not settle Sheehan. A proof that no 4-regular simple graph is uniquely Hamiltonian would not, by itself, give a fast algorithm for finding Hamiltonian cycles. The two subjects share drawings and share vocabulary. They do not share every theorem.

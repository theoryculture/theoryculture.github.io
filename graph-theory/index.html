Graph Theory

Basic definitions

Graph theory is the mathematics of points and the lines that join them. The language is small. Once the words below are in place, one can ask which routes exist, which cycles close, and when a cycle is the only one of its kind.

1. Graphs, vertices, and edges
Graph
A graph is a pair \( G = (V, E) \), where \( V \) is a finite set of vertices (also called nodes) and \( E \) is a set of edges. Each edge joins two vertices, called its ends.
Order and size
The order of \( G \) is \( n = |V| \). The size is \( m = |E| \).
Adjacent and incident
Two vertices are adjacent if an edge joins them. An edge is incident with its two ends. Two edges are adjacent if they share a vertex.
Loop and multiple edges
A loop is an edge whose two ends are the same vertex. Multiple edges are two or more edges with the same pair of ends.
Simple graph
A simple graph has no loops and no multiple edges. Then \( E \) may be treated as a set of unordered pairs \( \{u,v\} \) with \( u \neq v \).
Multigraph
A multigraph allows multiple edges (and, depending on the author, loops). The constructions around uniquely Hamiltonian 4-regular graphs are often stated for multigraphs.
Directed graph
In a directed graph (digraph) each edge is an ordered pair \( (u,v) \), an arrow from \( u \) to \( v \). Unless said otherwise, graphs below are undirected.
2. Degree and neighbourhood
Neighbourhood
The neighbourhood \( N(v) \) of a vertex \( v \) is the set of vertices adjacent to \( v \).
Degree
The degree \( d(v) \) (or \( \deg(v) \)) is the number of edges incident with \( v \), with a loop counted twice. In a simple graph, \( d(v) = |N(v)| \).
Minimum and maximum degree
\( \delta(G) = \min_v d(v) \) and \( \Delta(G) = \max_v d(v) \).
Isolated vertex and leaf
A vertex of degree \( 0 \) is isolated. A vertex of degree \( 1 \) is a leaf (or pendant vertex).
Handshaking lemma
The sum of all degrees is twice the number of edges: \[ \sum_{v \in V} d(v) = 2|E|. \] In particular, the number of odd-degree vertices is even.
Regular graph
\( G \) is \( r \)-regular if every vertex has degree \( r \). A 3-regular graph is also called cubic.
3. Special graphs
Complete graph \( K_n \)
Every pair of distinct vertices is joined by exactly one edge. \( K_n \) is \( (n-1) \)-regular and has \( \binom{n}{2} \) edges.
Path \( P_n \) and cycle \( C_n \)
\( P_n \) is a path on \( n \) vertices. \( C_n \) is a cycle on \( n \) vertices (\( n \ge 3 \)). Cycle graphs are 2-regular.
Bipartite graph
The vertex set splits as \( V = A \cup B \), \( A \cap B = \emptyset \), and every edge has one end in \( A \) and one end in \( B \). Equivalently, \( G \) contains no odd cycle.
Complete bipartite graph \( K_{s,t} \)
Every vertex of a part of size \( s \) is joined to every vertex of a part of size \( t \). The graph \( K_{1,n} \) is a star.
Empty graph
A graph with vertices but no edges. Sometimes written \( \overline{K_n} \).
Complement \( \overline{G} \)
Same vertex set as \( G \); two vertices are adjacent in \( \overline{G} \) if and only if they are not adjacent in \( G \).
4. Walks, trails, paths, and cycles
Walk
A sequence of vertices \( v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_k \) such that \( \{v_{i-1}, v_i\} \) is an edge for each \( i \). The length is the number of edges, \( k \).
Trail
A walk that repeats no edge.
Path
A walk that repeats no vertex. (Then it also repeats no edge.)
Closed walk
A walk with \( v_k = v_0 \) and \( k \ge 1 \).
Cycle
A closed walk of length at least 3 that repeats no vertex except the start and end. Two Hamiltonian cycles are regarded as the same when they use the same set of edges.
Circuit
A closed trail. An Eulerian circuit is a closed trail that uses every edge.
Distance
The distance \( \mathrm{dist}(u,v) \) is the length of a shortest \( u \)–\( v \) path, or infinite if none exists. The diameter is the greatest finite distance between two vertices.
5. Connectivity and trees
Connected
\( G \) is connected if there is a path between every pair of vertices. Otherwise it is disconnected.
Component
A maximal connected subgraph. Isolated vertices are components.
Bridge (cut edge)
An edge whose deletion increases the number of components.
Cut vertex
A vertex whose deletion increases the number of components.
Tree
A connected graph with no cycle. Equivalently: a connected graph on \( n \) vertices with \( n-1 \) edges; equivalently: a graph in which any two vertices are joined by a unique path.
Forest
A graph with no cycle (a disjoint union of trees).
Spanning tree
A subgraph that is a tree and uses every vertex of \( G \). Every connected graph has at least one.
6. Subgraphs and isomorphism
Subgraph
\( H = (V_H, E_H) \) is a subgraph of \( G \) if \( V_H \subseteq V \) and \( E_H \subseteq E \), with the ends of each edge of \( H \) lying in \( V_H \).
Induced subgraph
The subgraph \( G[S] \) on a vertex set \( S \subseteq V \) keeps every edge of \( G \) with both ends in \( S \).
Spanning subgraph
A subgraph with the same vertex set as \( G \).
Isomorphism
An isomorphism from \( G \) to \( H \) is a bijection \( f \colon V(G) \to V(H) \) such that \( \{u,v\} \) is an edge of \( G \) if and only if \( \{f(u), f(v)\} \) is an edge of \( H \). Then \( G \) and \( H \) are the same graph, drawn differently.
7. Eulerian and Hamiltonian graphs

These two notions ask for a tour of the whole graph, in different senses.

Eulerian circuit
A closed trail that uses every edge exactly once. A connected graph has an Eulerian circuit if and only if every vertex has even degree (Euler, 1736). The seven bridges of Königsberg fail this test.
Eulerian path
A trail (not necessarily closed) that uses every edge. It exists precisely when the graph is connected and has zero or two vertices of odd degree.
Hamiltonian cycle
A cycle that visits every vertex exactly once and returns to the start. A graph with such a cycle is Hamiltonian. There is no simple degree test analogous to Euler’s theorem.
Hamiltonian path
A path that visits every vertex exactly once. The name comes from Hamilton’s Icosian game on the dodecahedron; a closed knight’s tour of a chessboard is a Hamiltonian cycle in the knight’s graph.
8. Uniquely Hamiltonian graphs
Uniquely Hamiltonian
\( G \) is uniquely Hamiltonian if it has exactly one Hamiltonian cycle: exactly one set of edges that forms a cycle through every vertex.
Cycle graphs
Every \( C_n \) is uniquely Hamiltonian. These are the trivial 2-regular examples.
Sheehan’s conjecture (1975)
The only simple regular uniquely Hamiltonian graphs are the 2-regular ones. The odd-regular case is settled (Thomason). The even-regular case, from 4-regular graphs onward, is open.
Fleischner’s construction
From the Petersen graph one can build a uniquely Hamiltonian 4-regular multigraph, by way of an Eulerian \( (4,6) \)-regular graph. Whether the same construction works for other snarks is a separate question.
